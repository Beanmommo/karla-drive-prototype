# Helping your child learn to drive: Getting started

Reviewed: 28 September 2026.

Practical coaching takeaways from all six **Getting started → Supervisor** modules on [VicRoads myLearners](https://mylearners.vic.gov.au/). These notes focus on how parents can support learning, judge progress, and develop their child's independent decisions. The four-stage overview below comes from Getting started; the detailed Stage 1–4 modules are outside this review.

Example questions and suggested wording are original applications of the guidance, not quotations from VicRoads. The review covers the modules' written content; it does not add claims from embedded videos.

## 0.1 — What's first on their Ls

1. **Prepare them to make decisions when you are no longer beside them.** Supervised practice gives your child a supported environment in which to build experience before driving alone. When considering progress, think about the situations they have learned to handle and the help they still need. A smooth drive with your assistance is useful practice, but readiness for independent driving needs its own consideration.

2. **Treat hours as opportunities to learn.** Make regular time for practice and gradually broaden the situations your child experiences. Reaching an hours target should prompt a readiness discussion, not an automatic decision to book the test. The module encourages additional experience and agreement that the learner is ready.

Source: [What's first on their Ls](https://mylearners.vic.gov.au/Stages/Stage0/SD-First-on-Ls).

## 0.2 — Your role in safe driving

3. **Take responsibility for arranging useful practice.** Work with your child to choose drives suited to their current stage, make time for them, and gradually increase the challenge. Your contribution includes planning and feedback as well as accompanying them.

4. **Make your child a partner in the plan.** Decide together what they need to practise and discuss how it went. A practical opening question is: “What would you like more practice with today?” Use their answer alongside your observations to choose an appropriate drive.

5. **Remember that your own driving teaches them too.** The module asks adults to model safe behaviour. Apply that consistently when your child is a passenger: the habits and attitudes they see should support what you ask them to practise.

Source: [Your role in safe driving](https://mylearners.vic.gov.au/Stages/Stage0/SD-Your-role-in-road-safety).

## 0.3 — Why it's good to learn in stages

6. **Match challenges to readiness.** Allow repeated practice at your child's pace. Choose environments using this overview:

| Stage | Learning focus | What it means for the parent |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 — Car control | Starting, steering, stopping | Arrange quiet practice away from traffic. |
| 2 — Basic drives | Applying those skills around other road users | Use quiet roads at low speeds. |
| 3 — Complex drives | Busier traffic and varied hazards | Allow substantial practice with increasing complexity. |
| 4 — Rehearsing solo | Combining skills across roads and conditions | Gradually reduce your help as they prepare for independence. |

7. **Allow time for later stages.** Stage 1 is shortest; Stage 3 takes longer than Stages 1 and 2, and Stage 4 may take longest. Car control is an early milestone.

8. **Learn from an instructor.** Seek advice early about what and where to practise. Ask to accompany a lesson to improve your support. Choose someone your child works well with.

Source: [Why it's good to learn in stages](https://mylearners.vic.gov.au/Stages/Stage0/SD-Why-it-is-good-to-learn-in-stages).

## 0.4 — How to reduce your learner's risk of crashing

9. **Practise anticipation.** When appropriate for their stage, use real traffic to develop hazard awareness. Watch whether they recognise problems early enough to respond. Discuss what they noticed afterwards.

10. **Use crash scenarios as observation prompts.** Look for these decisions:

| Situation | What to notice as a supervisor |
| --- | --- |
| Traffic ahead changes | Do they notice early and adjust speed and space? |
| Turning right | Do they prepare early and judge the gap? |
| Another vehicle may turn across them | Do they consider whether its driver can see them and might fail to give way? |
| Bends or restricted visibility | Do they adjust before the difficulty, rather than react late? |

11. **Build experience across conditions gradually.** Use increasingly varied drives to practise these decisions at an appropriate level of difficulty. Treat an unfamiliar situation as something to learn with support.

12. **Use hazard-perception practice as an extra learning activity.** The module recommends the online practice test alongside on-road experience. Discuss the hazards your child recognised or missed.

Source: [How to reduce your learner's risk of crashing](https://mylearners.vic.gov.au/Stages/Stage0/SD-Reduce-learner-crash-risk).

## 0.5 — How to manage emotions on the road

13. **Check your own mood as well as theirs.** Your emotional state can affect their learning, memory, and decisions. Check in before practice. If either of you is tired, upset, or carrying tension from an argument, reschedule.

14. **Allow some nerves; respond to overwhelm.** Mild nervousness is normal when trying something new. Invite your child to tell you how they feel so you can judge whether they need reassurance, a break, or a different plan.

15. **Make it easy to ask you to take over.** Agree beforehand that they can request a break or hand the drive back when it becomes too complicated. For example: “Tell me if this feels too much. We can stop somewhere safe and swap.” This wording is a practical application of the module's advice.

16. **Keep mistakes useful for learning.** Stay calm, recognise what went well, and help them understand what to improve. Continue watching for hazards and supporting decisions while they learn. If stress becomes excessive, take a break rather than pressing on with the practice plan.

Source: [How to manage emotions on the road](https://mylearners.vic.gov.au/Stages/Stage0/SD-Manage-emotions).

## 0.6 — How to be a good supervising driver

17. **Find out what they are noticing and thinking.** Invite them to describe what they see and explain their choices. For example: “What did you notice that made you slow down?” Their explanation helps you understand the judgement behind the action. As a practical application, keep questions manageable and save longer discussion until parked if driving needs their full attention.

18. **Protect their attention while the basics are new.** Begin with a quiet learning environment. Introduce everyday distractions gradually as their experience grows. What feels effortless to you may still demand their concentration.

19. **Praise the safe decision specifically.** Help confidence grow alongside safe behaviour. Instead of only saying “good driving”, name what worked: “You noticed the traffic slowing early and gave yourself time.” This example applies the module's advice to give positive feedback.

20. **Finish with a short reflection led by your child.** Ask what they think went well and what they could have done differently. Use the conversation to plan subsequent practice. Example: “What felt comfortable today, and what would you like another go at?”

Source: [How to be a good supervising driver](https://mylearners.vic.gov.au/Stages/Stage0/SD-How-to-be-good-supervisor).
